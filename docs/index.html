If you had to rehearse a Mars mission tomorrow, where on Earth would you go?

Rovers and crews rehearse in deserts, craters and polar valleys long before they fly, and most of those places were chosen by experience. We compared 23 of them with 6 sites on Mars, using open NASA and ESA data, to find out which place fits which mission, and why.

Can you trust the rankings?

The method is never told which sites are famous Mars analogs. If it works, those should still score above places that are clearly not Mars-like, such as rainforest and farmland. AUC is the share of those pairs ranked correctly: 1.00 is perfect, 0.50 is a coin flip.

The thin line on each bar marks a coin flip. Weights were set before seeing any results. Landing tests score lowest, by design: on terrain alone, flat farmland resembles flat Mars plains.

Searching beyond the famous sites

Ranking famous sites is not enough, so we split the Negev, Sinai and southern Jordan into 444 squares of 20 by 20 km and scored every one of them. 100 squares outside our starting list score higher than the best of our 23 sites for at least one mission.

How our weekend went

We started on Saturday morning with a list of famous Mars analogs and one question: are they really the best places to practice?

By the afternoon, we had accidentally parked Curiosity on top of a 5 km mountain. We had typed in the middle of Gale Crater instead of the spot where the rover actually landed, and the middle of Gale Crater is Mount Sharp. We had also moved InSight about 1,100 km across Mars. Both are now back where they landed.

Then our first results told us that a Dutch farm field is a lot like Mars. On terrain alone, it is: both are extremely flat. So the landing briefs now check for bare ground too, and landing still has the lowest score in our validation. We kept it that way, because it is true.

Along the way, a Google form asked whether our project fights climate change. It does not. It looks for Mars on Earth.

Some answers we did not see coming. The best match for the plains where Opportunity and InSight landed is Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia, one of the flattest places on Earth. For practice drives near Jezero and Gale, the top matches were all in the Negev. And without being told it was famous, our method put the Atacama Desert first for life detection at every Mars target. We are taking that as a good sign.

No place on Earth has Mars's thin air, its cold nights or its radiation. We wrote those limits on every brief instead of leaving them out.
